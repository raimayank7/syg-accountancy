Depreciation: straight line and written down value methods

A machine bought today will be worth less each year until it is scrap. Depreciation spreads that loss fairly over the years the machine works.

Meaning

Depreciation is the fall in the book value of a tangible fixed asset because of use, the passing of time or obsolescence. Charging it each year does two things: it shows the true cost of earning that year's revenue (the matching principle), and it shows the asset at a sensible value in the balance sheet. Depreciation is a non-cash expense: no money is paid to anyone.

Related words: depletion is used for natural resources such as mines and quarries; amortisation for intangible assets such as patents. Land is normally not depreciated.

Causes and the three figures you need

  • Wear and tear from use.
  • Passage of time, as with a lease.
  • Obsolescence: a newer and better model makes the asset out of date.
  • Accidents and abnormal factors.

To calculate depreciation you need: cost (purchase price plus all expenses to bring the asset into use: freight, installation, trial run), estimated useful life, and estimated scrap (residual) value.

Straight line method (SLM)

The same amount is charged every year.

Annual depreciation = (Cost − Scrap value) ÷ Useful life

Rate = Annual depreciation ÷ Cost × 100

Example. On 1 April 2023 Deepak Industries bought a machine for ₹4,50,000 and spent ₹50,000 on installation. Its life is estimated at 5 years and scrap value at ₹50,000. Books close on 31 March.

Cost = ₹4,50,000 + ₹50,000 = ₹5,00,000. Annual depreciation = (5,00,000 − 50,000) ÷ 5 = ₹90,000, which is 18% of cost.

Written down value method (WDV, diminishing balance)

A fixed percentage is charged each year on the book value at the beginning of that year. The amount falls every year, and the book value never quite reaches zero.

In the balance sheet the provision is shown as a deduction from the cost of the asset: Machinery ₹5,00,000 less provision ₹2,70,000 = ₹2,30,000 after three years.

Sale of an asset

Example (SLM, continued). On 1 October 2026 the machine is sold for ₹1,70,000.

  1. Depreciation for the six months to the date of sale: ₹90,000 × 6/12 = ₹45,000.
  2. Book value on the date of sale: ₹2,30,000 − ₹45,000 = ₹1,85,000.
  3. Sale price ₹1,70,000 is less than book value, so the loss on sale is ₹15,000.

The same sale with a provision account: Asset Disposal Account

When the asset is kept at cost, a separate Machinery Disposal A/c collects the cost, the accumulated depreciation and the sale proceeds. Accumulated depreciation to the date of sale = ₹2,70,000 + ₹45,000 = ₹3,15,000.

Points to remember

  • Depreciation is charged from the date the asset is put to use. For an asset bought on 1 October, charge six months in the first year unless the question says otherwise.
  • Under WDV, "10% p.a." is always on the reducing balance, never on cost.
  • Additions during the year are depreciated only for the months they were held.
  • Repairs that keep the asset working are revenue expenses; they are not added to cost.

For real businesses. Rates and methods for tax and for company accounts are laid down by law and differ from textbook examples. The figures here are for learning the method.

Questions students ask

What is the formula for straight line depreciation?

Annual depreciation = (Cost − Scrap value) ÷ Useful life in years. Cost includes freight and installation.

Why is the written down value method suitable for machinery?

Because machinery loses more value in early years and needs more repairs later. Falling depreciation and rising repairs together give a fairly even yearly charge.

Is depreciation a cash expense?

No. It is a book entry that reduces profit and the asset's value. No cash is paid out.
