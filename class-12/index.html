Class 12 accountancy: all topics with solved examples

13 topics, each on its own page with the rules in plain words and a worked example whose figures have been checked by calculation.

  • Partnership fundamentals and the Profit and Loss Appropriation Account Two or more people sharing one business must agree how profit is divided before a single rupee is earned. The accounts simply carry out that agreement.
  • Goodwill: nature and methods of valuation An old, trusted shop earns more than a new one with the same stock and furniture. The value of that extra earning power is goodwill.
  • Change in profit sharing ratio among existing partners When partners change how they share future profits, the one who gives up a share must be paid for it by the one who gains. Everything earned so far is settled in the old ratio.
  • Admission of a partner, with a fully solved example A new partner buys two things: a share in the firm's assets, for which he brings capital, and a share in its future profits, for which he pays goodwill.
  • Retirement and death of a partner When a partner leaves, the firm must work out everything he is owed up to that day, pay him or his family fairly, and carry on.
  • Dissolution of a partnership firm When the firm itself closes, everything is sold, everyone outside is paid, and whatever remains is shared. One account, the Realisation Account, tells the whole story.
  • Issue, forfeiture and reissue of shares A company collects its capital in small equal units called shares, often in instalments. Each instalment has its own pair of entries.
  • Issue of debentures: journal entries for every case A share makes you an owner. A debenture makes you a lender. The company must pay interest on debentures whether it earns a profit or not.
  • Financial statements of a company (Schedule III) A company cannot arrange its balance sheet as it likes. The law fixes the headings and their order, so that any reader can compare any two companies.
  • Ratio analysis: formulas and a fully worked example A profit of one lakh is splendid for a tea stall and poor for a factory. Ratios turn raw figures into comparisons that mean something.
  • Cash flow statement (indirect method), solved A company can show a profit and still run out of cash. The cash flow statement shows where cash actually came from and where it went.
  • Comparative and common-size statements One year's figures say little by themselves. Put two years side by side, or turn every figure into a percentage of sales, and patterns appear.
  • Accounts of not-for-profit organisations A club or charity does not exist to earn profit, but its members still want to know: did this year's income cover this year's spending, and what do we own?

What Class 12 adds

Class 11 dealt with one owner. Class 12 asks what changes when ownership is shared. In a partnership, profit has to be divided, and every change in the partners (a new one joining, an old one leaving, the firm closing) means settling accounts between them fairly. In a company, ownership is divided into shares held by many people who never meet, and the law lays down how capital is raised and how the accounts are presented. The last part of the year turns from preparing accounts to reading them: ratios, comparative statements and the cash flow statement.

The partnership pages should be read in order, because each builds on the one before. Fundamentals gives the appropriation account and capital accounts. Goodwill gives the valuation methods. Change in ratio introduces the three adjustments (goodwill, reserves, revaluation) that then repeat on admission and retirement or death. Dissolution stands slightly apart: there the business ends, and the Realisation Account replaces the Revaluation Account.

The one idea behind reconstitution

Whenever the agreement between partners changes, everything earned or accumulated up to that day belongs to the old partners in the old ratio. That single sentence explains why reserves are distributed in the old ratio, why revaluation profit goes in the old ratio, and why the partner who gains a share pays goodwill to the partner who gives it up. If you can say that sentence and apply it, most questions on admission and retirement become mechanical.

Company accounts and analysis

For shares and debentures the entries follow the stages of collecting money: application, allotment, calls. Learn one full example by heart, including forfeiture and reissue, and vary it. For the analysis chapters, learn the Schedule III headings first; ratios and cash flow statements are easy to get wrong simply by placing an item under the wrong head. All formulas are gathered on the formula sheet.

How to revise

  • Keep a one-page list of formats: appropriation account, revaluation account, realisation account, cash flow statement.
  • Redo each solved example on this site on paper without looking, then check.
  • Time yourself: a full admission or cash flow question should fit within the minutes your paper allows for its marks.
  • Check every balance sheet you prepare: if it does not agree, find the error instead of forcing it.
