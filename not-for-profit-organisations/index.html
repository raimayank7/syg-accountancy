Accounts of not-for-profit organisations

A club or charity does not exist to earn profit, but its members still want to know: did this year's income cover this year's spending, and what do we own?

What makes these accounts different

Clubs, societies, schools, hospitals, temples and charitable trusts exist to serve their members or the public, not to earn profit for owners. Their main income is subscriptions, donations and grants, not sales. They have no owners' capital; the excess of assets over liabilities is called the capital fund or general fund. They prepare three statements at the year end:

  1. Receipts and Payments Account: a summary of the cash book.
  2. Income and Expenditure Account: the equivalent of a Profit and Loss Account.
  3. Balance Sheet.

Receipts and Payments Account

It records all cash received and paid during the year, whether capital or revenue, and whether for this year, last year or next year. It begins with the opening cash and bank balance and ends with the closing balance. It is a real account. It does not show income earned or expenses incurred, and it ignores non-cash items such as depreciation.

Income and Expenditure Account

It records only revenue items and only those of the current year, on the accrual basis: expenses on the debit side, incomes on the credit side. The balance is a surplus (excess of income over expenditure) or a deficit, which is added to or deducted from the capital fund. It is a nominal account.

Subscriptions: the adjustment

Subscription income for the year = Subscriptions received during the year − received for the previous year − received in advance for next year + outstanding for the current year + received last year in advance for this year.

What happened to each item

  • Sports equipment and investments purchased are capital payments: they went to the balance sheet, not to Income and Expenditure.
  • Life membership fees were capitalised and added to the capital fund.
  • The ₹5,000 of last year's subscriptions received this year cancelled the opening asset; it is neither income of this year nor in the closing balance sheet.
  • The surplus of ₹40,000 was added to the capital fund.

Stock of consumables: sports material, stationery, medicines

Amount consumed = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock, where purchases = cash paid to suppliers + closing creditors − opening creditors (adjusting for advances). Only the amount consumed is debited to Income and Expenditure Account.

Example: opening stock of stationery ₹3,000; paid for stationery ₹5,000; creditors for stationery rose from ₹1,000 to ₹1,500; closing stock ₹2,500. Purchases = 5,000 + 1,500 − 1,000 = ₹5,500. Consumed = 3,000 + 5,500 − 2,500 = ₹6,000.

Steps to convert Receipts and Payments into Income and Expenditure

  1. Leave out the opening and closing cash balances.
  2. Leave out capital receipts and capital payments; take them to the balance sheet.
  3. For each revenue item, adjust for last year's, next year's, outstanding and prepaid amounts.
  4. Add non-cash items: depreciation, profit or loss on sale of assets.
  5. Find the opening capital fund from an opening balance sheet.
  6. Balance the account: surplus or deficit goes to capital fund.

Questions students ask

What is the difference between Receipts and Payments Account and Income and Expenditure Account?

The first is a cash summary recording all receipts and payments of any nature and any period. The second records only the current year's revenue income and expenses on the accrual basis and shows surplus or deficit.

How are life membership fees treated?

As a capital receipt. They are added to the capital fund in the balance sheet and not credited to the Income and Expenditure Account.

What is the capital fund?

The excess of a not-for-profit organisation's assets over its liabilities. Each year's surplus is added to it and each deficit deducted.
