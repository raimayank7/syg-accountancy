Accounting basics: meaning, process and basic terms

Accounting is the language a business uses to tell its own story in numbers. Learn the words first and every later chapter becomes easier.

What accounting is

A shopkeeper buys, sells, pays rent, takes loans and gives credit all day long. Memory cannot hold all of it, and memory cannot prove anything to a bank or a tax officer. Accounting is the orderly way of recording these money events, classifying them under heads, summarising them into statements and interpreting the result, so that the owner and others can take decisions.

Book-keeping is the first part of this work: the routine recording and posting. Accounting is wider: it includes preparing the final statements and reading what they mean.

The accounting process, step by step

  1. Identify the transaction. Only events measurable in money are recorded. A manager's skill is valuable but is not a transaction.
  2. Record it in the book of original entry, the journal, from a source document such as a bill or receipt.
  3. Classify by posting to accounts in the ledger, so that all entries about one person or thing are in one place.
  4. Summarise: balance the accounts, draw up a trial balance, then the final accounts.
  5. Analyse and interpret the statements, for example with ratios.
  6. Communicate the result to those who need it.

Who uses accounting information

UserWhat they want to know

Basic terms, each with an example

The example business is Meera Stores, a stationery shop owned by Meera.

TermMeaningAt Meera Stores
CapitalMoney or goods the owner puts into the business. The business owes it to the owner.Meera brings in ₹5,00,000 to start the shop.
DrawingsCash or goods the owner takes out for personal use.Meera takes ₹5,000 from the till for a family function.
AssetsThings of value the business owns. Non-current (fixed) assets are for long use; current assets turn into cash within a year.Furniture is a fixed asset. Stock, debtors and cash are current assets.

GoodsThings bought to sell again.Notebooks and pens are goods. The shop counter is not.

SalesGoods sold, for cash or on credit.Notebooks sold to a school for ₹70,000.
Stock (inventory)Goods lying unsold on a given date.Unsold pens on 31 March are closing stock.
DebtorA person who owes money to the business for goods sold on credit.The school, until it pays its bill.
CreditorA person to whom the business owes money for goods bought on credit.Gupta Traders, until Meera pays them.

Branches and limits

Financial accounting records transactions and reports profit and financial position. Cost accounting finds the cost of a product or service. Management accounting prepares information for decisions inside the business.

Accounting has limits. It records only what can be measured in money. It mostly shows assets at what they cost, not what they are worth today. And it depends on estimates, such as the life of a machine, so two honest accountants can show slightly different profits.

Basic assumptions and principles

  • Business entity: the business is separate from its owner. That is why capital is shown as a liability of the business.
  • Money measurement: only transactions expressed in money are recorded.
  • Going concern: the business is assumed to continue for the foreseeable future.
  • Accounting period: the life of the business is cut into periods, usually a year from 1 April to 31 March in India.
  • Cost: assets are recorded at the price paid for them.
  • Dual aspect: every transaction has two sides of equal value. This gives the accounting equation.
  • Matching: expenses of a period are set against the revenue of the same period.
  • Accrual: income and expenses are recorded when earned or incurred, not when cash moves.
  • Consistency and prudence: use the same methods year after year; provide for likely losses but do not count gains before they are earned.

Questions students ask

What is the difference between book-keeping and accounting?

Book-keeping is the routine recording of transactions in the journal and ledger. Accounting includes book-keeping and goes further: summarising, preparing final accounts and interpreting them.

Is capital an asset or a liability?

For the business, capital is a liability, because the business owes that amount to the owner. This follows from the business entity assumption.

What is the difference between a debtor and a creditor?

A debtor owes money to the business. A creditor is owed money by the business. Debtors are an asset; creditors are a liability.
