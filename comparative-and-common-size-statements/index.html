Comparative and common-size statements

One year's figures say little by themselves. Put two years side by side, or turn every figure into a percentage of sales, and patterns appear.

Analysis of financial statements

Financial statement analysis means examining the figures in the statements to judge the profitability, efficiency and financial soundness of a business. The main tools are:

  • Comparative statements (horizontal analysis): figures of two or more years side by side, with the change in rupees and in per cent.
  • Common-size statements (vertical analysis): every item shown as a percentage of a base figure of the same year.
  • Ratio analysis.
  • Cash flow statement.

Comparative statement

It has five columns: Particulars; Previous year; Current year; Absolute change; Percentage change.

  • Absolute change = Current year figure − Previous year figure.
  • Percentage change = Absolute change ÷ Previous year figure × 100.

Example. Prepare a comparative Statement of Profit and Loss of Tapti Ltd.

Reading it

  • Revenue from operations grew by 25%.
  • Cost of materials and employee cost grew by only 20%, and other expenses by about 11%. Total expenses rose by about 19%, more slowly than revenue.
  • Because expenses grew more slowly than income, profit before tax rose by 50%, twice the growth in revenue.
  • Conclusion: the company has not only sold more but has controlled its costs better.

Common-size statement

In a common-size Statement of Profit and Loss, revenue from operations is taken as 100 and every other item is expressed as a percentage of it. In a common-size balance sheet, the total of the balance sheet is taken as 100.

Other expenses90,0001,00,0009%8%
Total expenses8,40,00010,00,00084%80%
Profit before tax2,00,0003,00,00020%24%
Tax (30%)60,00090,0006%7.2%
Profit after tax1,40,0002,10,00014%16.8%

Reading it

  • Materials took 60 paise of every rupee of revenue last year and 57.6 paise this year.
  • Employee cost fell from 15% to 14.4%, and other expenses from 9% to 8%.
  • Total expenses fell from 84% to 80% of revenue, so profit before tax rose from 20% to 24%.
  • Profit after tax improved from 14% to 16.8% of revenue.

The comparative statement told us how much each item changed. The common-size statement tells us how the make-up of each rupee of revenue changed. The two are read together.

The two compared

BasisComparative statementCommon-size statement
Also calledHorizontal analysisVertical analysis
ComparesThe same item across yearsDifferent items within one year
ShowsChange in rupees and per centEach item as a percentage of a base
NeedsAt least two years' figuresOne year is enough; more years add value
Best forGrowth and trendStructure, and comparing firms of different size

Steps for the examination

  1. Draw the columns and write the items in the Schedule III order.
  2. Fill in the two years' figures; work out sub-totals (total income, total expenses, profit before and after tax).
  3. For a comparative statement, compute change and percentage for every line, including sub-totals.
  4. For a common-size statement, divide every line by revenue from operations (or by the balance sheet total) and multiply by 100.
  5. Round percentages to two decimal places and say so.

Limits of these tools

  • They rest on the financial statements and share their limits: historical cost, estimates and policy differences.
  • A percentage on a tiny base can look dramatic and mean little.
  • They show what changed, not why. The reasons must be sought in the business itself.

Questions students ask

What is the base in a common-size statement of profit and loss?

Revenue from operations is taken as 100, and every other item is shown as a percentage of it.

How is percentage change calculated in a comparative statement?

Absolute change divided by the previous year's figure, multiplied by 100.

Why is a comparative statement called horizontal analysis?

Because the eye moves across the columns, comparing the same item from one year to the next.
