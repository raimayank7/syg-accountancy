Class 11 accountancy: all topics with solved examples

17 topics, each on its own page with the rules in plain words and a worked example whose figures have been checked by calculation.

  • Accounting basics: meaning, process and basic terms Accounting is the language a business uses to tell its own story in numbers. Learn the words first and every later chapter becomes easier.
  • The accounting equation, with a solved example Whatever a business owns was paid for by someone: an outsider or the owner. That one idea is the whole equation.
  • Rules of debit and credit Debit means the left side of an account and credit means the right side. Nothing more. The rules only tell you which side an increase goes on.
  • Journal entries, with solved examples The journal is the diary of the business: every transaction, in date order, with its debit, its credit and a line of explanation.
  • Ledger posting and balancing of accounts The journal tells the story in date order. The ledger sorts the same story account by account, so you can see where each one stands.
  • Cash book: single column, double column and petty cash The cash book is a journal and a ledger account at once. Record cash here and you need neither a journal entry nor a separate Cash A/c.
  • Subsidiary books: purchases, sales and returns When a business has hundreds of credit purchases and sales, one journal is not enough. The journal is split into special books, one for each kind of transaction.
  • Trial balance: preparation and solved example If every debit had an equal credit, the list of all debit balances must total the same as the list of all credit balances. The trial balance tests exactly that.
  • Bank reconciliation statement (BRS), solved Your cash book says ₹50,000 is in the bank. The bank says ₹54,000. Both can be right. The reconciliation statement explains the gap.
  • Rectification of errors and the Suspense Account An error is corrected by an entry, never by rubbing out. First work out what was done, then what should have been done; the difference is the correction.
  • Depreciation: straight line and written down value methods A machine bought today will be worth less each year until it is scrap. Depreciation spreads that loss fairly over the years the machine works.
  • Provisions and reserves A provision is set aside because something is expected to cost money. A reserve is kept back to make the business stronger. One is a charge, the other is a choice.
  • Bills of exchange: entries in the books of drawer and drawee A bill of exchange turns a spoken promise to pay later into a signed paper that can be held, sold to a bank or passed on to someone else.
  • Final accounts of a sole proprietor, solved A year of entries comes down to two questions. How much did the business earn? What does it own and owe today? The final accounts answer both.
  • Adjustments in final accounts, with a solved example An adjustment is a fact given after the trial balance. Because it is not yet in the books, it must be entered twice, once on each side of the double entry.
  • Accounts from incomplete records (single entry system) Many small traders keep only a cash book and a note of who owes whom. Profit can still be found, by comparing what the owner was worth at the start and at the end.
  • GST journal entries, with solved examples GST collected on sales is not income, and GST paid on purchases is usually not an expense. Both sit in tax accounts until they are set off against each other.

How Class 11 accountancy is built

Class 11 is where the subject begins, and it is the year that decides how comfortable you will be later. Everything rests on one idea, the dual aspect: every transaction touches two accounts by the same amount. The year takes that idea through the whole accounting cycle: a transaction is recorded in the journal, posted to the ledger, summarised in a trial balance, and finally turned into a statement of profit and a statement of what the business owns and owes.

The pages above follow that order. The first three (basics, the accounting equation, debit and credit rules) are the foundation; do not hurry through them. The middle group (journal, ledger, cash book, subsidiary books, trial balance) is the cycle itself, and the same small business, Arjun Traders, is followed from journal to trial balance so that you can see the figures carry forward. After that come the special topics, each of which answers one practical question: why does the bank show a different balance, how are mistakes corrected, how is a machine's cost spread over its life, what is a bill of exchange. The year ends with final accounts, first without adjustments and then with them.

How to study a topic

  1. Read the explanation once, slowly.
  2. Cover the solution of the solved example and attempt it yourself on paper, with a ruler and proper columns.
  3. Compare line by line. Where you differ, find out why before moving on.
  4. Do five similar questions from your own textbook the same day.
  5. Come back after a week and redo the solved example without looking.

Accountancy is a practical subject. Reading solutions feels like understanding, but the skill is built only by writing entries and drawing accounts yourself. The practice quiz gives quick drill in deciding debit and credit, which is the step that every later topic depends on.

Common difficulties in Class 11

  • Debit and credit feel arbitrary. Learn the five kinds of account and one rule for each; see rules of debit and credit.
  • Posting on the wrong side. The account debited in the journal is posted on its own debit side, naming the other account.
  • Adjustments. Remember that each adjustment appears twice; see adjustments.
  • Presentation. Marks are lost for missing narrations, dates, "To" and "By", and untidy totals. Practise the format as carefully as the figures.
